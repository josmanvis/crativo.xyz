I skate. I also write code for a living. For a long time I treated those as two separate parts of my life: one involves concrete and the occasional bruise, the other involves TypeScript and the occasional existential crisis.

But the longer I do both, the more they look like the same hobby wearing different shoes.

## Falling is the feedback loop

Nobody learns a trick without falling. Not "almost nobody." Nobody. Every slam is information: your weight was too far back, your front foot flicked too early, you looked down instead of ahead.

That's debugging. A failing test, a stack trace, a broken layout on Safari: each is the board shooting out from under you and telling you exactly what you did wrong, if you're willing to listen instead of getting mad at the concrete.

The skaters who progress aren't the ones who fall the least. They're the ones who fall, get up, change *one thing*, and try again. Change one variable at a time. That's the scientific method, but with more grip tape.

## Reps beat inspiration

You don't land a trick by watching videos of it. You land it by trying it a lot of times, most of which look terrible.

Code works the same. Reading about a framework feels productive. Building something small with it, breaking it and fixing it is what actually puts it in your hands. There's no shortcut through the reps, and honestly, the reps are the fun part.

## Commit, or eat it

The fastest way to get hurt skating is to try a trick halfway. You hesitate, you don't commit, and you end up in the worst possible position: neither landing nor bailing safely.

Half-committed engineering hurts in the same way. Half a migration. Two state management libraries because nobody finished moving off the first. A redesign that only got applied to three pages. Either commit to the change or don't start it. The in-between is where people get hurt.

## Know how to bail

Good skaters learn how to fall before they learn tricks: how to step off, how to roll out, how to protect their wrists. Bailing is a skill.

In software, that's rollbacks, feature flags, backups and undo. Ship boldly, but always know your bail. The engineers I trust most aren't the ones who never break production. They're the ones who know exactly how to get out when they do.

## Style comes from constraints

Every skate spot has limits: a short run-up, a crusty ledge, a weird bank. Skaters don't wait for the perfect park. They find the line that works with what's there, and that's where style comes from.

Most of the best interfaces I've built came from constraints too: a TV remote with six buttons, a watch with a two-second attention span, a kiosk that strangers have to understand instantly. The limits are where the interesting decisions live.

## Nobody cares about your trick list

Here's the most freeing thing about skateboarding: the skatepark doesn't care how long you've been skating. It only cares whether you're trying. A kid on their first day and someone with twenty years in will cheer for each other's landings the same way.

Software culture could use more of that. Less "how many years of experience," more "nice, you landed it."

## Go skate

If you're stuck on a bug, go outside and try to learn something physical. It resets your brain, it reminds you that being bad at something is the first step of being good at it, and you'll probably come back with the fix.

Also, wear wrist guards. Seriously. You type for a living.
